1. Hook: the dataset that disappeared

A student finishes a UAV-LiDAR maize project. The paper says "data available upon request." Two years later a reviewer asks for it. The student has graduated. The Google Drive link points to a folder named final_v3_REAL/ with 40 .laz files, no flight altitude, no plot map, no note on which genotype sat in which row. The data still exists. It is just useless.

This is not rare. A 2018 European Commission study (done by PwC) estimated that data that is not FAIR costs the European economy at least €10.2 billion per year, plus a further ~€16 billion in effects they could not measure directly.

And it is now a funding rule in the U.S. The 2022 OSTP "Nelson memo" told every federal agency (USDA included) to make publications and their supporting data public with no embargo, with policies in effect by December 31, 2025. Any USDA-NIFA or NSF award you win from here on will expect a real data plan.

FAIR is the recipe for making data survive the student who made it.

2. Intuition: a library for datasets

FAIR comes from Wilkinson et al., Scientific Data (2016). It has four letters and 15 sub-principles. The key idea: data should be usable by humans and machines. A script, a search engine, or an AI agent should be able to find and load your data without emailing you.

Think of a public library:

Letter Library version Your lab version
F – Findable Every book has a call number and a catalog card Every dataset has a DOI (a permanent ID) and rich metadata (data about the data) in a searchable index
A – Accessible Clear rules for checking a book out, and the catalog card stays even if the book is lost Data comes back over a standard protocol (HTTPS). Login is allowed. The metadata stays online even if the data is removed
I – Interoperable Books are written in a language readers know Standard file formats (LAS, PLY, ENVI, VCF) and shared vocabularies (ontologies: agreed lists of terms with IDs, such as Crop Ontology trait IDs)
R – Reusable You know the edition, the author, and the copyright A license (e.g. CC-BY 4.0), provenance (how the data was made), and a community standard (MIAPPE for plant phenotyping)

FAIR does not mean open. Your Corteva genotype data can stay private and still be FAIR. The metadata is public, the DOI resolves, and the access rule ("request through data-use agreement") is written down. The EU slogan: "as open as possible, as closed as necessary."

flowchart LR
  A[Raw capture: UAV LiDAR, turntable HSI, SNP chips] --> B[Organize: folder layout, file naming, open formats]
  B --> C[Describe: MIAPPE sheet, Crop Ontology IDs, Croissant JSON-LD]
  C --> D[License and provenance: CC-BY, pipeline git hash, calibration]
  D --> E[Deposit: repository mints a DOI]
  E --> F[Test: F-UJI score, fix gaps]
  F --> G[Cite: DOI in paper and data availability statement]
  • Raw capture: sensors produce files. Nothing is FAIR yet.
  • Organize: predictable names and open formats so a stranger can navigate.
  • Describe: metadata that a machine can parse, using shared terms.
  • License and provenance: who may reuse it and how it was made.
  • Deposit: a repository gives the permanent ID (F1) and keeps metadata online (A2).
  • Test: an automated checker tells you which sub-principles fail.
  • Cite: the DOI goes in the paper, so the data earns citations.

3. The math: scoring FAIRness

3a. The 15 sub-principles as a checklist

ID Short meaning
F1 Globally unique, persistent ID (DOI)
F2 Rich metadata
F3 Metadata names the data's ID
F4 Registered in a searchable index
A1 Retrievable by ID over a standard protocol
A1.1 Protocol is open and free (HTTPS)
A1.2 Protocol allows login/authorization when needed
A2 Metadata stays even if data is gone
I1 Formal, shared language for knowledge (JSON-LD, RDF)
I2 Vocabularies that are themselves FAIR (ontologies)
I3 Qualified links to other (meta)data
R1 Many accurate, relevant attributes
R1.1 Clear usage license
R1.2 Detailed provenance
R1.3 Meets domain community standards

Give each sub-principle a score $s_k \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}$ (fails, partly, passes). This is the same idea automated tools like F-UJI use, with more detailed tests.

3b. Letter scores and overall score

$$ F = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{F}} s_k,\quad A = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{A}} s_k,\quad I = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{I}} s_k,\quad R = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{R}} s_k $$

$$ \text{FAIR\%} = \frac{100}{15}\sum_{k=1}^{15} s_k $$

  • Symbols: $s_k$ = score of sub-principle $k$; $\mathcal{F}$ = the set {F1–F4}, etc.
  • Intuition: the average fraction of boxes you ticked.
  • Geometry: each dataset is a point in a 15-D cube $[0,1]^{15}$. FAIR% is how far along the main diagonal from the "nothing" corner to the "perfect" corner.

3c. The weakest-link model (why an average hides failure)

Reuse is a chain: someone must find it AND access it AND read it AND trust it. If each step succeeds with probability equal to its letter score, and the steps are roughly independent:

$$ P(\text{reuse}) \approx F \cdot A \cdot I \cdot R $$

  • Symbols: $F, A, I, R \in [0,1]$ are the letter scores from 3b.
  • Intuition: AND-logic multiplies. One zero kills the chain.
  • Geometry: $F\cdot A\cdot I\cdot R$ is the volume of a 4-D box with those side lengths. Squash any side to zero and the volume is zero.

Why this matters: a dataset with perfect A, I, R but no DOI or index ($F = 0$) has an average of $(0+1+1+1)/4 = 0.75$, which looks "75% FAIR." But $P(\text{reuse}) = 0 \times 1 \times 1 \times 1 = 0$. Nobody can reuse data they cannot find.

Caveat: this product model is a teaching model, not a published standard. It shows the logic that each letter is required, not a measured probability.

3d. The payoff model

Colavizza et al. (PLOS ONE, 2020) studied 531,889 PLOS and BMC papers. Papers whose data availability statement linked to data in a repository had up to 25.36% (± 1.07%) higher citation impact on average:

$$ C_{\text{with repo}} \approx C_{\text{base}} \times 1.2536 $$

This is an association, not proof of cause. A 2024 follow-up by the same group confirmed a positive link but found a smaller effect.

4. Worked example: your UAV-LiDAR maize dataset

Before (typical lab state): point clouds on Google Drive, a README with flight notes, link in the paper.

ID Score Reason
F1 0 Drive link, not a DOI. Links break when accounts close
F2 0.5 README has some details, missing altitude, sensor, plot map
F3 0 No metadata record points to an ID
F4 0 Google Dataset Search cannot index a private Drive folder
A1 0.5 Downloadable if the link still works
A1.1 1 HTTPS is open and free
A1.2 0.5 Sharing is on/off, no documented access rule
A2 0 Delete the folder and all trace is gone
I1 0.5 LAZ is a standard format, but README is free text
I2 0 No ontology terms ("plant height" with no definition or ID)
I3 0 No link to genotype file, weather data, or paper
R1 0.5 Some attributes present
R1.1 0 No license. Legally, nobody may reuse it
R1.2 0.5 Partial processing notes
R1.3 0 No MIAPPE, no Croissant

After (steps in Section 7): deposited in a repository with a DOI, MIAPPE sheet, Crop Ontology trait IDs, Croissant JSON-LD, CC-BY 4.0 license, pipeline git hash, links to genotype and paper. Two items stay at 0.5: I2 (not every custom 3D trait has an ontology term yet) and R1.3 (MIAPPE has no field-level standard for 3D point-cloud acquisition).

5. Numerical example (verified in Python)

F A I R FAIR% (mean of 15) $P(\text{reuse}) = F\cdot A\cdot I\cdot R$
Before 0.125 0.500 0.167 0.250 26.7% 0.0026
After 1.000 1.000 0.833 0.875 93.3% 0.729

Step by step, before: $0.125 \times 0.5 = 0.0625$, then $\times 0.1667 = 0.01042$, then $\times 0.25 = 0.0026$.

Step by step, after: $1 \times 1 = 1$, then $\times 0.8333 = 0.8333$, then $\times 0.875 = 0.729$.

  • The mean went up about 3.5× (26.7% → 93.3%).
  • The weakest-link product went up 280× (0.0026 → 0.729).

That gap is the lesson. Fixing the worst letter (F, from 0.125 to 1) does more than polishing the best one.

Citation payoff: if a paper would get 20 citations with data "on request," the Colavizza association suggests about $20 \times 1.2536 \approx 25$ with data in a repository.

7. Why, How, What: implementing FAIR in your lab

Why

Funders now require it (Nelson memo, USDA public access plan). NeurIPS Datasets tracks require Croissant metadata, and for 2026 also Responsible-AI fields. Reviewers of your AFRI proposal will read the Data Management Plan. And it saves your own lab time when a new student picks up an old dataset.

How: an 8-step playbook

1. Plan at proposal time. Write the DMP while writing the AFRI proposal. Name the repository, formats, license, and who is responsible. Budget storage (3D + hyperspectral is terabytes).

2. Folder layout and names. One pattern for every project:

maize-uav-lidar-2025/
  raw/        # never edited
  processed/  # point clouds, traits
  metadata/   # MIAPPE sheet, croissant.json, calibration
  code/       # or a pinned git commit hash
  README.md

File names: ISU-AEA_2025-07-14_plot0213_lidar.laz — site, ISO date, plot, sensor. No spaces, no "final".

3. Open, standard formats.

Data Format
LiDAR / SfM point clouds LAS/LAZ (with CRS), PLY
NeRF / 3DGS outputs PLY splats + transforms.json camera poses
Hyperspectral cubes ENVI (.hdr + .bil) or HDF5, wavelength list inside
Genotypes VCF (+ reference genome version)
Trait tables CSV or Parquet, one row per plant/plot
Images PNG/TIFF, with EXIF kept

4. Metadata with shared terms. - MIAPPE 1.1 (Minimum Information About a Plant Phenotyping Experiment): investigation, study, biological material, environment, observed variables. - Crop Ontology IDs for traits, so "plant height" means one defined measurement. - ORCID for people, ROR for ISU, grant number for the funder. - Croissant JSON-LD for anything ML-ready (VGGT training sets, turntable splats).

Minimal Croissant sketch:

{
  "@context": {"@vocab": "https://schema.org/", "cr": "http://mlcommons.org/croissant/"},
  "@type": "Dataset",
  "name": "ISU maize UAV-LiDAR 2025",
  "description": "Plot-level LiDAR point clouds of 480 maize hybrids, Ames IA, 3 dates.",
  "license": "https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/",
  "identifier": "https://doi.org/10.xxxx/placeholder",
  "creator": [{"@type": "Person", "name": "Zaki", "identifier": "https://orcid.org/..."}],
  "distribution": [{"@type": "cr:FileSet", "encodingFormat": "application/vnd.las", "includes": "processed/*.laz"}]
}

(Plot count and DOI are placeholders; replace with the real values.)

5. License. CC-BY 4.0 for data (reuse allowed with credit). MIT or BSD for code. For restricted data (industry genotypes), write the access rule in the metadata and use the repository's restricted-access option.

6. Provenance. Record sensor model and firmware, flight altitude and overlap, RTK base, calibration panel, software versions, and the git commit hash of the processing pipeline.

7. Deposit and get a DOI. Options: ISU's institutional repository (ISU DataShare), Zenodo (links to GitHub releases), CyVerse Data Commons (large plant data), Hugging Face (auto-generates Croissant core fields), NCBI SRA / MaizeGDB for sequence data. Very large raw data can live on your HPC storage with a smaller DOI'd subset plus a documented access route.

8. Test and cite. Run the DOI through F-UJI (open-source, from FAIRsFAIR). Fix what fails. Put the DOI in the paper's data availability statement and reference list.

What you get

A dataset that a stranger (or a script) can find, load, and trust without you. That is the full definition of success.

8. Implications for your work

  • AFRI 3D + genomics proposal: a strong DMP naming MIAPPE, Croissant, and a DOI repository is a scoring point, and the Nelson-era USDA policy expects it.
  • VGGT / IMECE 2026 and future dataset papers: if you target a NeurIPS Datasets track, Croissant + RAI fields are mandatory, not optional.
  • Genomic prediction with private data: FAIR lets you publish the metadata and model code while keeping genotypes restricted (A1.2).
  • Hyperspectral turntable + GS-PI: without wavelength lists, illumination, and calibration in the metadata, a cube is uninterpretable. Interoperability (I) is where hyperspectral data most often fails.
  • Multi-modal linking (I3): linking a plot's LiDAR, HSI, and VCF by shared IDs is what makes a 3D + genomics platform queryable instead of a pile of files.
  • AI agents: FAIR was written for machines as users. Machine-readable metadata is what lets an agent find and load your data automatically.

9. Current research landscape

  • Wilkinson et al. (2016), Scientific Data 3:160018: the original 15 principles.
  • Papoutsoglou et al. (2020), New Phytologist: MIAPPE 1.1, a data model and ontology for plant phenotyping; implementations in ISA-Tab, RDF (PPEO), and BrAPI.
  • BrAPI (Breeding API): a standard REST API so breeding databases can exchange phenotypic and genotypic data. It covers the Accessible part MIAPPE does not.
  • FAIR reuse case study (2023), Scientific Data s41597-023-02364-z: re-FAIRifying old potato field trials with MIAPPE showed that metadata not recorded during the experiment is very hard to recover later.
  • Devaraju & Huber (2021), Patterns: F-UJI automated FAIR assessment. Petrosyan et al. (2023), Ecological Informatics: applied F-UJI to agricultural datasets.
  • Akhtar et al. (2024), NeurIPS D&B: Croissant metadata for ML datasets, built on schema.org. Croissant Baker (2026, arXiv 2605.15079): generating Croissant for large local repositories that cannot be uploaded to public platforms, which matches the HPC-scale 3D data problem.
  • FAIR 2.0 (arXiv 2405.03345): argues the original principles under-specify semantic interoperability and proposes extensions.
  • Colavizza et al. (2020, 2024), PLOS ONE: citation advantage for repository-linked data, smaller in the 2024 follow-up.

Open gap relevant to you: there is no mature community standard for describing 3D plant reconstructions (camera rigs, NeRF/3DGS settings, point-cloud trait extraction). MIAPPE covers the experiment, not the 3D acquisition. This is a publishable contribution and a natural topic for your planned NIFA 3D phenotyping workshop.

10. Five-year projection (2026–2031)

These are projections from the trends above, not established facts.

  • 2026–2027: Most federal awards enforce no-embargo data sharing. DMP quality becomes a routine review item. More ML venues follow NeurIPS in requiring Croissant.
  • 2027–2028: Automated FAIR checkers (F-UJI style) get built into repositories and submission systems, returning a score at upload.
  • 2028–2029: Metadata drafting by AI agents becomes normal: an agent reads your pipeline logs and fills MIAPPE and Croissant fields for you to review.
  • 2029–2031: Community standards emerge for 3D and hyperspectral plant data. Cross-lab training of foundation models on FAIR phenotyping data (multi-site 3D + genomics) becomes practical because the data can be found and joined automatically.

Quick self-check

  1. Your dataset has a DOI and license but trait columns are named ht, lai2, x7. Which letter fails most? (I: I2, and R1)
  2. Can private Corteva genotypes be FAIR? (Yes. Public metadata, DOI, documented access rule: A1.2)
  3. Why does the product model punish a missing DOI more than the mean does? (AND-logic: one zero side gives zero volume)
